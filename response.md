## 1. Section summaries

### Abstract
The paper claims that language models are stuck making token-by-token, left-to-right decisions, which hurts on tasks needing exploration or lookahead. It proposes Tree of Thoughts (ToT), which searches over "thoughts" (coherent chunks of text) instead of tokens, and reports large gains on three hard tasks — most strikingly 74% vs. 4% for chain-of-thought on Game of 24.

### 1. Introduction
Instead of just generating text, an LM should be able to *deliberate*: keep several options alive, judge how promising each one is, look ahead, and back up when stuck. The framing comes from dual-process psychology (fast "System 1" vs. slow "System 2") and from Newell, Shaw & Simon's 1950s view of problem solving as search through a tree of partial solutions. The key novelty claimed is that the *search heuristic itself* is now written in language by the model, rather than programmed (DeepBlue) or learned (AlphaGo).

### 2. Background
Formalizes the methods ToT is compared against: **IO** prompting (input → output directly), **CoT** (insert a chain of intermediate thoughts between input and output), and **CoT-SC** (sample many chains and take the majority answer). Two weaknesses are set up here: CoT never explores alternatives *inside* a chain, and CoT-SC's majority vote only works when the output space is small.

### 3. Tree of Thoughts (the framework)
The core section. Any problem becomes a tree where a node is a state `s = [x, z1...zi]` (input plus thoughts so far), and using ToT means answering four design questions:

- **Thought decomposition** — what counts as one step. A thought should be small enough that the model can generate several *diverse* ones, but big enough that the model can *judge* one. In the paper: a word (crosswords), an equation (Game of 24), a paragraph-long plan (creative writing).
- **Thought generation** — two strategies: **sample** k independent thoughts from a CoT-style prompt (good when the space is rich, e.g. paragraphs), or **propose** k thoughts together in one prompt (good when the space is constrained, e.g. single words, so you avoid duplicates).
- **State evaluation** — two strategies: **value** each state on its own (a score like 1–10, or a label like sure/likely/impossible), or **vote** across states by comparing them and picking the best. Both can be sampled multiple times for robustness.
- **Search** — **BFS**, keeping the best b states per step (used for the shallow tasks, depth ≤ 3), or **DFS**, going deep on the best state and pruning when the evaluator says it's hopeless, backtracking otherwise.

Also claims four benefits: generality (IO, CoT, CoT-SC, and self-refinement are all just trees of limited depth and width), modularity (every piece is swappable), adaptability (tune to task and budget), and convenience (no extra training).

### 4. Experiments
Three tasks built to be hard for GPT-4, all with temperature 0.7.

**4.1 Game of 24** — use four numbers and arithmetic to reach 24. Thoughts are the three intermediate equations; generation is by *propose*; evaluation is *value* with sure/maybe/impossible sampled 3 times; search is BFS with b = 5. Results: IO 7.3%, CoT 4.0%, CoT-SC 9.0%, ToT 74% (b = 5) and 45% even with b = 1. Best-of-100 CoT only reaches 49%. The error analysis is one of the most interesting findings: about 60% of CoT failures already happen at the first step, i.e. after the first three words.

**4.2 Creative writing** — write four paragraphs ending in four given sentences. Thoughts are a short writing plan; generation is by *sample* (5 plans), evaluation is by *vote* (5 votes), depth 2, breadth 1. Results: GPT-4 coherency 6.19 (IO) → 6.93 (CoT) → 7.56 (ToT), and human raters preferred ToT over CoT in 41 of 100 pairs vs. 21 the other way (38 ties). Notably, plain **iterative refine** did best of all on this task (7.67 IO, 7.91 ToT), which the authors say is effectively a third kind of thought generation.

**4.3 Mini crosswords** — 5×5 crosswords, used as a deeper search problem. Thoughts are a word placed for a clue, generated by *propose* with confidence levels aggregated into a priority queue; evaluation checks whether each remaining clue is still fillable and prunes if any is "impossible"; search is DFS with backtracking and a 100-step cap. Results: ToT gets 78% of letters, 60% of words, and solves 4/20 games, vs. IO/CoT under 16% word-level and zero games. An oracle best-state variant solves 7/20. Ablations: removing pruning drops to 65.4% letter / 41.5% word, removing backtracking collapses to 54.6% / 20% — but without pruning it actually *finds* correct answers on 4 games, 3 of which pruning cannot solve, i.e. the heuristic sometimes prunes the right answer.

### 5. Related work
Positions ToT against LM planning work, self-reflection/self-critique methods, program-guided decoding, and classical search. Closest neighbors: RAP (concurrent MCTS over LM reasoning, but less modular), self-eval guided decoding (tree search but thoughts-as-code, so it can't do creative writing), and A* (ToT is like A* where the heuristic is the LM's own self-assessment).

### 6. Discussion
Limitations: ToT is unnecessary for tasks GPT-4 already handles, the three tasks are relatively simple, and search costs 5–100× more generated tokens than CoT (Game of 24: $0.74/case vs. $0.47 for best-of-100 CoT). Future work: better search algorithms (A*, MCTS), cheaper setups, and *fine-tuning* models to make high-level, deliberate choices instead of next-token predictions.

### Broader impact / Appendix
ToT both increases risk (more autonomous decision-making) and improves interpretability, since the reasoning is readable language rather than opaque token values. Appendix B adds three useful extras: a generic zero-shot ToT helps only slightly on GSM8K (86 → 90) and StrategyQA (82 → 83); GPT-3.5 gets just 19% on Game of 24 vs. GPT-4's 74%, and mixing models shows generation quality matters far more than evaluation quality (GPT-4 generation + GPT-3.5 evaluation = 64%, but the reverse = 31%); and a cost table.

### The combined questions these overlaps produce

**C1. (O1) What is a "thought," really — and how much does choosing that unit decide the outcome?**
Merges: thought as a unit of reasoning / how the unit affects performance / decomposing a reasoning trace into a thought / whether decomposition injects human priors / designing good intermediate steps / tasks where a clear thought unit is hard to find.
*Why it's one question:* every one of these is asking the same thing from a different angle — who decides the granularity, and what does that decision cost? The paper's two-sided rule ("small enough to generate diverse samples, big enough to evaluate") is a circular constraint: you need thoughts in order to evaluate thoughts. Neither list noticed this circularity, which makes it a strong opening question.
*Paper anchors:* Table 1 (word / equation / paragraph); Game of 24's 60%-of-CoT-failures-at-step-1 error analysis; crosswords where a thought is one word.

**C2. (O2) How do we know an evaluation is good — and is "good" even well-defined?**
Merges: how to define a good evaluation metric / is sure-maybe-impossible too coarse or too fine / what is the creative-writing evaluator evaluating / what biases does the evaluator carry.
*Why it's one question:* both lists treat evaluation as the framework's weak joint, one worrying about *resolution* (3 labels vs. a score) and one about *validity* (measuring the right thing). They are the same worry: an evaluator can be high-resolution and still wrong.
*Paper anchors:* sure/maybe/impossible sampled 3×; GPT-4-as-judge coherency scores with standard deviation ≈ 0.56; the crosswords puzzle where the evaluator calls a word impossible in a game that is actually solved; GPT-4 judge scoring its own family of outputs.

**C3. (O3) Sample vs. propose — is "rich vs. constrained" a real rule or a post-hoc story?**
Merges: which is more important / which for what situation / tradeoffs / are there theoretical guarantees / what if differently-worded outputs are the same idea.
*Why it's one question:* Andy's objection (diversity may be cosmetic) is exactly what would falsify Cecilia's practical rule (sample when the space is rich). Testing whether samples are *semantically* different, not just lexically different, tests both at once.
*Paper anchors:* sample used for creative writing, propose for Game of 24 and crosswords; the note that propose "avoids duplication" in constrained spaces; temperature 0.7 everywhere.

**C4. (O4) Value vs. vote — and can they be combined on purpose?**
Merges: value vs. vote tradeoffs / combining value for the first half and vote for the second half.
*Why it's one question:* Andy's hybrid idea is the natural experiment that answers Cecilia's tradeoff question. The paper picks one per task and never mixes them, so the hybrid is genuinely untested and cheap to discuss.
*Paper anchors:* value with lookahead simulations + commonsense on Game of 24; vote on creative writing, where success "is harder to directly value"; Table 3's "-backtrack" ablation at 20% word-level.

**C5. (O5) Pruning: how do you know you pruned something good — and what does the crosswords ablation tell us?**
Merges: premature pruning and undo / is pruning too greedy / exploration vs. exploitation balance.
*Why it's one question:* the paper's own ablation is the answer and the problem at the same time. Removing pruning *hurts* aggregate performance (65.4% → 41.5% word-level) but finds correct solutions on 4 games, 3 of which pruning can never solve. That single result contains both sides of the debate.
*Paper anchors:* DFS prunes when any remaining clue is "impossible"; "-prune" and "+best state" rows of Table 3; backtracking ablation 54.6% / 20%.

**C6. (O6) BFS vs. DFS: a property of the task, the model, or the budget?**
Merges: is BFS-vs-DFS task-specific or worth comparing per task / which is better for which task type.
*Why it's one question:* the paper's stated reason for BFS on two tasks is "the tree depth is limited (T ≤ 3)" — that is a *task shape* argument, not a task *content* argument. A good discussion topic is whether the real driver is depth, branching factor, how binary the evaluator's signal is, or how much the user is willing to pay.
*Paper anchors:* BFS b = 5 for Game of 24 and creative writing; DFS with a 100-step cap for crosswords; best-of-k scaling curve in Figure 3(a) used as the "nodes visited" comparison.

**C7. (O7) Is ToT clever, or is it just spending more tokens?**
Merges: unfair comparison given combinatorially more compute / is there something clever or are we just consuming tokens.
*Why it's one question:* this is the paper's central methodological challenge and it appears in both lists almost verbatim. It deserves to be settled (or at least sharpened) early because every other question about search quality depends on the answer.
*Paper anchors:* Figure 3(a) compares ToT against IO/CoT "best of k" by mapping k samples to nodes visited — so best-of-100 CoT = 49% vs. ToT = 74%; Table 7 shows ToT costs $0.74/case vs. $0.47 for best-of-100 CoT; Table 8 shows ~5× tokens on creative writing. The honest counter-argument: ToT's 45% at b = 1 uses far fewer nodes than best-of-100 CoT's 49%.

**C8. (O8) Which tasks does ToT actually suit, and were these three tasks chosen to prove the point?**
Merges: are the three tasks engineered toward ToT's advantage (with the three specific reasons Andy gives) / would more or fewer intermediate steps help creative writing / how to design intermediate steps.
*Why it's one question:* the "is this rigged?" critique and the "would differently-sized steps work better?" question are the same conversation — both are about how well the task's structure matches the framework's assumptions. Note that the paper half-concedes: it says crosswords are "readily solved with specialized NLP pipelines" and that it deliberately picked tasks CoT fails.
*Paper anchors:* the three bullet reasons in Andy's list map onto the three tasks one-to-one; Appendix B.1 shows only +4 and +1 point gains on GSM8K and StrategyQA.

**C9. (O9) Is the "human-like" approach the right target at all?**
Merges: is the characteristically human approach necessarily best / what limitations and advantages do humans and agents have that push toward different approaches / what biases does the evaluator carry.
*Why it's one question:* the paper's motivation is explicitly cognitive-science-flavored (System 1 / System 2, Newell-Simon search). If we take that seriously, we should ask which human shortcuts are worth copying and which are just our own hardware limits — and whether an LM's very different constraints (perfect recall, cheap resampling, no fatigue, but a weak and possibly biased self-judge) imply a different optimal strategy.
*Paper anchors:* the Introduction's dual-process framing; the quote from Newell et al.; the claim that LM self-evaluation is a "third alternative" to programmed or learned heuristics.

### Coverage map

Every question in both original lists maps into C1–C9 except two that are genuinely standalone: Andy's question about how temperature affects sampling, and his question about whether LM state reasoning could be replaced by something like JEV (a learned value/judgment model). Those two are not merged into C1–C9; the second reappears as additional question 7, and the temperature question is left for whoever wants to raise it, since it is really a sub-case of C3.

## 3. Discussion sets

### Cecilia's 8 questions

**1. Who actually chooses what a "thought" is — the task, the model, or us?**
The paper says a thought should be small enough to generate diverse samples and big enough to evaluate, and then assigns the granularity by hand per task: a word for crosswords, one equation for Game of 24, a paragraph for creative writing. That rule is circular — you need a thought in order to judge whether the granularity works. So is thought decomposition a genuine design choice, or a human prior we are smuggling in and then measuring ourselves against? And is there a principled way to search over granularities rather than picking one per task?

**2. Sample or propose — when is diversity real diversity?**
The rule offered is "sample when the thought space is rich, propose when it's constrained." On creative writing, five sampled plans might be five rewordings of one idea; the paper never checks whether the samples differ in *substance* rather than surface form. How would you measure that? And does the rule survive contact with a task where the space is rich but the model is confidently wrong in the same direction five times?

**3. Value or vote — what would a hybrid look like, and where would you switch?**
Value scores each state alone; vote compares states against each other. Value seems better early (with few states there's nothing to compare against, and lookahead checks like "5+5+14 can reach 24" are available), and vote seems better late (when the thing being judged — passage coherency — is easier to compare than to score absolutely). If you built a value-then-vote hybrid, what's the switch criterion, and how would you know it beat either pure approach?

**4. How would you detect a premature prune, and is "undo" really possible?**
The crosswords ablation is the sharpest evidence in the paper: without pruning, word-level success drops from 60% to 41.5% — yet the no-prune run *finds* correct solutions on 4 games, 3 of which the pruning version cannot solve in 100 steps. So pruning simultaneously helps on average and destroys wins. What signal would tell you mid-search that you had just pruned a good branch? Is backtracking an "undo," or only an undo of the *choice* while the evaluator that made the mistake stays exactly as biased as before?

**5. Is BFS vs. DFS a property of the task, the evaluator, or the budget?**
The paper's justification for BFS on two tasks is that "the tree depth is limited (T ≤ 3)" and that initial steps can be pruned to b ≤ 5 — a claim about the shape of the tree, not its content. For crosswords, depth forces DFS. If you had a task with a fixed shallow depth but a very noisy evaluator, which would you pick? What about deep but cheap-to-evaluate? Try to state the choice rule the paper only implies.

**6. Is sure / maybe / impossible the right resolution, and what does it mean to be more certain?**
Three labels, sampled three times per thought, with `maybe` doing all the heavy lifting as the catch-all. Compare two situations: one vote for "sure" plus two for "impossible," versus two votes for "maybe" with nothing else. Which state should the search keep, and why? Does the answer change if the three samples are correlated (same prompt, same model, so they probably share the same blind spots)?

**7. On creative writing, what exactly is the score measuring — and whose taste is it?**
Two evaluators are used: a GPT-4 zero-shot 1–10 coherency score, and a blind human comparison run by the authors. Coherency is not creativity, and "ends in the four given sentences and hangs together" is a constraint-satisfaction measure with a literary flavor. What would a better measure of this task look like, and what would it have to control for — length, surprise, grammaticality, or simply the fact that the judge and the generator are the same model family with the same stylistic defaults?

**8. Refinement beat ToT on creative writing. What does that tell us about where ToT earns its keep?**
Iterative refine alone got IO from 6.19 to 7.67, higher than ToT's 7.56, and refine on top of ToT reached 7.91. The authors note refine is effectively a third kind of thought generation (improving a thought rather than resampling or proposing a new one). So on open-ended tasks, is the win from *searching* or from *iterating*? On which kind of task would refinement be useless and search essential — and does Game of 24 fit that description?

### Andy's 8 questions

**1. Before we discuss any result — what is a good evaluation metric for a ToT-style method?**
The paper's metrics are success rate (Game of 24), a GPT-4-judged coherency score plus a human pair comparison (creative writing), and letter/word/game accuracy (crosswords). Success rate is unambiguous; the others are proxies. If you were designing the evaluation from scratch, what would it need to capture that these don't — for example correctness per unit of compute, robustness to prompt wording, or whether the method wins for the *right* reason?

**2. How much of ToT's advantage is attributable to its own design, versus the tasks being shaped to fit it?**
The three tasks map suspiciously cleanly onto the framework's three assumptions: Game of 24 needs several candidate solutions held in mind (so BFS with a beam pays off), creative writing needs several plot options explored (so sampling plans pays off), crosswords need conditioning on other slots' possible answers (so backtracking pays off). The paper also concedes crosswords are solvable by specialized pipelines and that it chose tasks CoT fails. If you swapped in a task that needs long *depth* rather than breadth, or where a wrong early decision is cheap to fix, would ToT still look this good?

**3. Is search over a probabilistic LM the right mechanism at all?**
ToT conducts its search using a next-token predictor as both generator and heuristic, then asks the same model to judge its own outputs. That means the search's promise and its errors come from the same distribution. Is that a strength (self-consistent, no training) or a structural flaw (correlated failures that no amount of extra sampling fixes)? And does the appendix result — GPT-4 generation with GPT-3.5 evaluation gets 64%, while the reverse gets 31% — support the paper's claim that "generation is the bottleneck," or does it mainly show that evaluation is only useful when it's already good?

**4. Decomposing reasoning into thoughts: engineering choice or smuggled human prior?**
"One equation," "one word," "one paragraph" are decisions a human makes about what counts as a unit of reasoning, and they are made with knowledge of the answer format. Is that privileged knowledge in any meaningful sense — the same kind of advantage a trained reward model would give? Are there problems where a clean thought unit genuinely doesn't exist (a proof, a long debugging session, a negotiation) and ToT simply cannot be applied?

**5. Is ToT clever, or is it compute in a nicer costume?**
ToT uses 5–100× more tokens than CoT, and the fairest comparison in the paper maps best-of-k IO/CoT onto "nodes visited": best-of-100 CoT gets 49%, ToT gets 74%. But ToT at b = 1 already gets 45%, which looks like a real win from *structure* rather than volume — while CoT (best of 100) beating CoT-SC (k = 100, 9%) suggests volume without structure is nearly worthless on this task. So is the honest claim "structure beats volume at equal volume," and does the paper demonstrate that, or only the weaker claim that more compute helps?

**6. Is the "characteristically human" framing doing real work, or is it decoration?**
The paper leans on System 1 / System 2 and on Newell and Simon's tree search to motivate the design. Humans also use pattern recognition, analogy, and insight that look nothing like search, and humans search under constraints an LM doesn't have (bounded working memory, costly deliberation, fatigue) while an LM has cheap resampling and perfect recall. If we designed deliberation around the LM's actual constraints instead of ours, would the resulting method still look like a tree, or like something else entirely — a large parallel proposal step with no explicit tree at all?

**7. Are three evaluator labels and a fixed beam a hidden hyperparameter problem?**
b = 5, 3 value samples, 5 votes, 5 plan samples, a 100-step DFS cap, depth 2, temperature 0.7, and thresholds like `vth` are all chosen per task without a stated selection procedure. If performance is this sensitive to them, how much of the reported result is the framework and how much is tuning? Which of these would you expect to transfer across tasks, and how would you tell the difference between "the framework is robust" and "the authors tuned it well on the test set"?

**8. Where does ToT stop being worth it — and what replaces it?**
The appendix concedes almost no gain on GSM8K (86 → 90) and StrategyQA (82 → 83), and refinement beats ToT on creative writing. Meanwhile ToT is where CoT collapses (Game of 24: 4% → 74%). That suggests a boundary: ToT pays when a wrong early commitment is expensive and the model can tell better from worse locally. Can you state that boundary as a testable prediction? And on the other side, when would you rather spend the same compute on a longer single chain, on reflection, on retrieval, or on sampling more strategies?

## 4. Additional questions

1. **Are we still grading ToT on tasks today's models have already solved?** Game of 24 was chosen because GPT-4 with CoT scored 4%. Later models score far higher on it with plain CoT. If a task becomes easy, ToT's apparent advantage shrinks toward the appendix's GSM8K result (+4 points). What is the right benchmark *now* for deliberate search — and should the field re-run Table 2 on current models before citing 74% vs. 4%?

2. **The evaluator's errors are correlated, not random — does the theory assume otherwise?** Value and vote are sampled multiple times for robustness, which only helps if errors are independent. The same model, prompt, and task mean the three samples likely share the same blind spot (as with "agend" being read as a typo for "agenda"). So what does "sampling for robustness" actually buy — lower variance, or only the appearance of it?

3. **"Impossible" is the most dangerous label in the paper.** `maybe` is safe (keep it), `sure` is cheap (expand it), but `impossible` deletes a subtree permanently in DFS. Should the pruning threshold be a *budget* decision — prune the worst branch only when resources run out — rather than a *truth* decision about the branch?

4. **What would ToT's cost look like if it were honest about stopping?** The paper recommends early stopping when a solution is found and trimming the beam when thoughts are impossible, but the reported Game of 24 runs never stop early. If ToT stopped as soon as a valid equation appeared, how would its token cost and success rate compare to best-of-k CoT — and would the comparison still favor ToT?

5. **Is "the tree" doing the work, or is "many proposals plus a good judge" doing the work?** Game of 24 uses BFS; crosswords use DFS; creative writing is depth 2 with breadth 1, which is barely a tree. If you removed the tree and just sampled k candidates at each step, kept the best, and never backtracked, how much would you lose? The "-backtrack" ablation (20% word-level) hints the answer on crosswords — but does anything in the paper test it on Game of 24?

6. **Does ToT improve interpretability in the way the Broader Impact section claims?** The claim is that readable thoughts make decisions easier to audit. But the branch that produced the final answer is selected by a *vote* — often a majority of sampled votes — not by an explicit argument. Can a user reconstruct *why* the chosen thought won, or does the audit trail stop at "the model said choice 3 was best"?

7. **Could the search heuristic be trained instead of prompted — and if so, does the framework's "no training needed" selling point survive?** The paper's own future-work section suggests fine-tuning for high-level deliberate choices. If a small trained value model replaced the prompted evaluator, would that be better (calibrated, cheap, reusable) or worse (task-specific, no longer general-purpose)? The GPT-4-generation + GPT-3.5-evaluation result (64%) suggests evaluation is a candidate for cheap substitution.

8. **What is the smallest change that would have broken the results?** For instance: swapping the sure/maybe/impossible value prompt for a 1–10 score, changing the beam from 5 to 3, or using one value sample instead of three. The paper does not report a sensitivity analysis for most of these. Ask the group to predict which one matters most and why — prediction before evidence is the fastest way to surface how well everyone has internalized the mechanism.

9. **Where do humans and LMs differ in ways that should change the algorithm, not just its speed?** Humans backtrack partly because working memory decays; LMs can hold the entire tree cheaply, so they should arguably favor *breadth* far more than humans do. Humans also recognize a good idea instantly without articulating why; LMs need the judgment written in language, which is exactly why ToT's heuristic is a prompt. Which parts of the human search analogy are load-bearing and which are just metaphor?

10. **If the LM can judge a state, why does it need a tree at all?** A judge good enough to rank partial solutions accurately is, in principle, close to a judge good enough to rank complete ones — in which case you could sample widely and pick once. The framework's value therefore rests on judgments being *locally* reliable but *globally* insufficient. Is that assumption stated anywhere, and is it ever tested — for example, does judging accuracy degrade as the states get deeper?

## 5. Suggested flow for the discussion

If you have about an hour: open with **C7 / Andy Q5** (is this just compute?) because it frames everything else, spend the middle on **C5 + C6** (pruning, undo, and search choice) where the paper's own ablations provide the most concrete disagreement, then close with **C1 + C9** (what a thought is, and whether human-style search is the right target) to push from the paper's mechanics toward its assumptions.
